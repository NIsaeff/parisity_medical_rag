Chunk overlap: why repeat yourself?

Some answers need two neighboring sentences: one names the thing, the next says what happened to it (“started lisinopril” then “two weeks later, a cough”). If a chunk boundary falls between them, no single chunk holds the whole answer. Retrieval returns a chunk that says “a cough” with no drug, or “he fed swine” with no idea who “he” is. Overlap repeats the last few sentences of each chunk at the start of the next, so neighbors can't be separated. Sentence overlap repeats whole sentences, so it never cuts one in half like character overlap can.

Use overlap when…

  • You slice flowing text by size, such as long reports, transcripts, contracts or discharge summaries. Ideas run across your arbitrary boundaries.
  • Sentences lean on the ones before them, through words like “it”, “he”, “this medication” or “the above”.
  • Answers span a few sentences. Overlap of 1–2 sentences, about 10–20% of the chunk, covers most of them. It guarantees two facts land together only if they're at most overlap sentences apart.

Skip it when…

  • Chunks follow natural, self-contained units, such as one clinical note = one vector, one Bible chapter, or one FAQ entry. The author already put the boundaries in the right place.
  • Duplicates would crowd your top-K. The same sentence stored twice can come back twice and take up a slot.
  • A cheaper fix exists. Prepend context such as Luke 15 or Patient: Jane D., 2024-03-02 to every chunk, so “he” and “it” have something to point at without duplicating text.
